>
> **SUSTech-BIO | Emit: start a conversation → Echo: listen to feedback → Evolve: put improvements into practice**
>
> Starting with clinicians' advice, older adults' questions and participants' feedback, we explore how RNA editing research can respond to real needs.

## 0. Introduction: Life has more than one bandwidth

“What can we do with this once we have extracted it?”

During a science outreach activity at Taoyuan Subdistrict Elderly Service Center, we guided older adults through a banana DNA extraction experiment. As the activity drew to a close, one participant asked this question. We had prepared scientific explanations and experimental steps, but still needed to address her central concern: how did this activity relate to her life?

Her question prompted us to reconsider the starting point of science outreach and became an important guide to our understanding of Human Practices (HP). What experiences do older adults, patients, caregivers and members of the public bring? How might they view the technology we are studying, and how should their questions shape our next steps?

Our project, **ORCA**, explores a therapeutic RNA editing strategy for Alzheimer's disease (AD). We aim to alter a specific site in APOE4 mRNA through C-to-U editing, shifting its protein product towards an APOE3-like form. APOE ε4 is associated with increased AD risk, but carrying this allele does not mean that someone already has the disease.[1] ORCA remains at the research stage. We combine wet-lab experiments with computational modelling to investigate editing efficiency, specificity and delivery. Related RNA editing research has also informed our choice of evaluation criteria.[2]

To understand the needs this concept would encounter, we interviewed clinicians and researchers and asked elderly service center staff about older adults' daily lives. Through curriculum comparisons, surveys and public activities, we also explored how to explain complex scientific questions clearly. Expert advice informed experimental evaluation, while feedback from older adults and center staff guided workbook revisions. Older adults also became users testing our mini-app and contributors to activity design.

Using orca echolocation as a metaphor, we describe this process as **Emit–Echo–Evolve: start conversations with questions, listen carefully to responses and turn feedback into improvements.** Participants also contribute knowledge and experience as “givers”. Our concept of “inclusion across the spectrum” recognizes that people understand information and express their views differently, and provides corresponding opportunities to participate. Some prefer reading, others need illustrations or physical objects, and others raise questions from everyday experience. The following account documents these exchanges and how they shaped ORCA.

## 1. Develop ORCA: Bringing real needs into project design

Moving RNA editing from a research concept towards future applications requires answers to questions beyond the laboratory. Clinicians can explain difficulties in diagnosis and care, while researchers can identify gaps in evaluation plans. Caregivers and potential users can draw attention to the burdens of long-term use. These perspectives helped us progressively define the questions ORCA needs to address.

When we introduced our RNA editing concept, Dr. Zhou emphasized **drug delivery**. How the editing system could cross the blood–brain barrier and reach relevant brain tissue remains a key question for future application. ### 1.2 Informed consent and proxy decision-making: Designing multiple prototype versions

Clinical interviews, discussions of individual choices and fieldwork in care settings led us to a shared concern. **Providing information does not mean it has been understood; obtaining a signature does not mean someone has received adequate decision support.** We treated this as a communication problem requiring further investigation and improvement. How can people from different backgrounds find information relevant to them and understand its meaning?

We therefore proposed a prototype that presents information in layers. A concise summary would explain the study purpose, stage, potential benefits, risks and alternatives, followed by illustrations, detailed explanations and a question-and-answer entry point. Large-print, audio and assisted-reading versions still require development and testing against participants' actual needs. Design intentions alone cannot establish that accessibility has been achieved.

The prototype should also include “understanding checkpoints”, asking readers to explain key information in their own words and reveal where the material remains unclear. These checks should improve explanations, without becoming barriers that exclude people with limited literacy or difficulties expressing themselves. An informed-consent puzzle game is one educational tool we plan to explore; Section 2.4 describes the concept.

For supported or proxy decision-making, we consider the individual's information needs separately from those of caregivers. Caregivers need to understand study arrangements, potential burdens and uncertainties, alongside the wishes the individual has already expressed. **Older age, cognitive impairment or a preference for family accompaniment should not automatically be equated with a lack of decision-making capacity.** In actual research, decisions concerning capacity, appropriate representation and the individual's wishes must follow applicable ethical and professional requirements. A prototype can support communication but cannot replace that process.[3]

### 1.3 Product development and commercialization: Translational questions and future interviews

Moving RNA editing from research to application also involves delivery, manufacturing, clinical evidence, payment and continuing services. For ORCA, product evaluation must extend beyond technical advantages to identify potential users, the support they need and unresolved costs and responsibilities.

We identified RNA and AI entrepreneurs, developers of cognitive-risk screening products, older-adult service professionals and home-adaptation providers as important contributors to translational discussions. Each group can help examine different assumptions. How should screening information be explained, how can services earn trust, can recommendations be implemented, and who will maintain a product over time? These questions form a framework for subsequent translational interviews and evaluation.

## 2. Extend Knowledge: Bringing people from different backgrounds into scientific discussion

Conversations with older adults and caregivers showed us that clear scientific explanations depend on understanding people's existing knowledge, concerns and preferred ways of learning. We therefore explored curricula, public understanding and specific use contexts, applying the feedback to workbooks, tools and activities.

### 2.1 Curriculum comparisons and teacher interviews: Where should synthetic biology education begin?

Before introducing synthetic biology, we needed to understand what students had already learned. High school biology provides foundations for understanding genes and life processes, but how often can students experience system design, experimental validation and improvement? To explore this question, we worked with **CUHK-HongKong-SBS** and **HG-Tokyo** to compare curricula and examination materials.

We compared four curriculum configurations: **Pearson Edexcel iAL Biology, Hong Kong HKDSE Biology, mainland Chinese high school biology and the gaokao, and Japanese high school biology combined with AP Biology**. The combination of Japanese high school biology and AP was a comparison configuration selected for this study.

Together, we developed a reference framework to organize materials across six domains. These were molecular biology foundations; core biotechnology tools; synthetic biology concepts; DBTL thinking; data, modelling and research literacy; and bioethics, biosafety and society. We compared curricula and examined how their requirements appeared in examination questions.

**The comparison identified strong existing foundations in molecular biology, data interpretation and experimental reasoning, with room to expand engineering design and complete iterative processes.** Biological parts, modularity, genetic circuits and the full design–build–test–learn (DBTL) cycle appeared less often as explicit learning frameworks. Ethical issues were already addressed, while risk assessment and discussions involving multiple stakeholders offered further opportunities for development.

These findings helped define our educational priorities: build on students' existing knowledge and use concrete problems to explain design, evidence evaluation and subsequent improvement. We also created a **web-based simulation** of an “exam-question transfer test” to explore how questions connect with different curricular backgrounds. Its design will be documented separately.

**Inside the classroom: advice from two high school teachers.**

Curriculum documents describe teaching requirements, while teachers' experience helps explain how these are implemented in classrooms. We therefore also spoke with high school biology teachers.

**Jiaju Li, Head of Biology Teaching and Research at Yali High School in Changsha**, described substantial training in scientific thinking, variable control and chart analysis. Recent Hunan gaokao biology questions increasingly use authentic research contexts. However, although textbooks cover techniques such as PCR and electrophoresis, opportunities for hands-on practice or system simulations remain limited. Examination pressure and lesson schedules are important constraints on practical teaching.

For an introduction to synthetic biology, Mr. Li suggested starting with **genetic engineering and protein engineering**, using project-based learning (PBL). His example was “improving natural insulin”: first examine stability and duration of action, then propose improvements, discuss how to test them and revise accordingly. This showed us how textbook knowledge could be organized into a complete problem-solving process.

For ethics teaching, he recommended real cases that prompt students to discuss evidence, risks and benefits, and informed consent. Different classroom settings also require different materials. Short cases and worksheets can support regular lessons, while clubs or longer projects allow more extensive exploration.

**Sijie Wang, a biology teacher at Yali High School**, added practical implementation advice. She noted that students already have foundations in controlling variables, setting controls and interpreting charts, but many design and improvement exercises remain on paper. She suggested around 12–15 lessons within a school-based elective for second-year high school students, combining cases, worksheets and simulated experiments. Environmental protection, fermentation engineering or microbial cultivation could serve as starting topics.

The curriculum comparison and teacher interviews together clarified our educational direction. **Start with familiar problems, connect existing knowledge to design, evidence evaluation and iterative improvement, and offer materials at different depths to fit classroom time.**

### 2.2 Public understanding survey: Addressing what people want to know first

Which information most needs explanation when introducing RNA editing, and what do people consider first when evaluating an emerging intervention? To explore these questions, we collected **103 valid survey responses** covering familiarity with the technology, research scenarios, decision-making information needs and preferred communication formats.

Among respondents, 96 were aged 18–30, accounting for **93.2%**; 5 had direct experience related to cognitive disorders or caregiving. We therefore mainly used the findings to understand younger respondents' views. Older adults' and caregivers' needs were explored separately through in-person exchanges.

**Respondents prioritized practical improvement, risks and the intended population.** Actual improvement mattered to 71 respondents (68.9%), serious adverse effects and their likelihood to 56 (54.4%), and the intended population to 48 (46.6%). This indicated that explanations should address early what a technology aims to improve, its potential risks and who is being studied.

The survey also identified wording that could cause confusion. One multiple-choice question described a hypothetical “40% average reduction in a biomarker”. In response, 72 participants (69.9%) selected the need to observe symptoms and long-term outcomes, while 31 (30.1%) selected that relatively clear clinical benefit had already been shown. These options could overlap, highlighting the importance of explaining what an indicator means. The 40% reduction was a hypothetical scenario in the question.

**How information is expressed also affects understanding.** Among respondents, 49.5% reported that saying only “effective”, without explaining what improved, hindered understanding; 44.7% selected excessive technical terminology. Illustrations or flowcharts, one-page summaries and short videos were selected by 51.5%, 49.5% and 45.6%, respectively.

We consequently prioritized four questions in communication materials: what improves, how large are the risks, who is it for, and what stage has the evidence reached? We chose a design direction of **providing concise summaries and illustrations first, followed by detailed information**. The survey clarified what needed explanation and which issues to examine during subsequent material trials.

### 2.4 An informed-consent puzzle game: Practising understanding and questioning through scenarios

Finding a sentence in research information is not always the same as understanding it. Building on our earlier reflections on risk communication, we proposed an **informed-consent puzzle game** as an educational tool. It would let participants practise finding information, identifying questions and explaining the basis for their judgments within a scenario.

The current concept begins with reading study materials and identifying the purpose, research stage, risks and alternatives. Participants would then discuss what information is missing and review which explanations are clear or potentially misleading. Plain text, illustrations and audio are possible formats for future development and comparison.

The concept focuses on whether participants can ask questions and explain their reasoning. Specific rules and testing arrangements will be refined as prototype development proceeds.

### 2.5 WORLDPROOF Hackathon: Challenging and Revising Designs

<div class="hp-media-row" markdown="1">
<div class="hp-media-copy" markdown="1">

Discussions of synthetic biology applications raise questions beyond the underlying technology: who will use it, who will maintain it, and what happens when something goes wrong? Participants from different disciplines may draw on their experience to identify issues that designers have overlooked.

We therefore organized the **WORLDPROOF Synthetic Biology Real-World Hackathon**, under the theme **“Build it. Break it. Fix it.”** Participants developed proposals while experiencing the process of challenging and revising them.

</div>

<figure class="hp-figure" id="hp-figure-26">

**We invited four judges from research, industry, and the iGEM community:**

- **Elzbieta Gralinska**: Lead of the iGEM Software & AI Village and Chief Scientist at Roche.
- **Dr. Yannan Tian**: Co-founder of RiboNext.
- **Jacky Yuen**: PI of the Chinese University of Hong Kong iGEM team.
- **Duncan Chiu**: Chair of the Greater Bay Area SynBio Alliance (iGBA) and iGEM Ambassador for Asia and Oceania.

The judges brought expertise in software and artificial intelligence, biotechnology research, industrial translation, and iGEM project supervision. These backgrounds offered different perspectives during evaluation and professional feedback to help participating teams reconsider and revise their projects.

**Every team completed a full cycle of prototyping, red-team critique, revision, and change-log documentation.** Teams first built a demonstrable prototype, which other teams examined from a red-team perspective to identify potential problems. The original team then revised its proposal and recorded what changed and why.

We designed this format to bring feedback into the design process. Participants could raise questions about cost, operation, communication, maintenance, and public trust. The proposing teams needed to respond carefully and explain their adjustments. This process also brought iterative design–build–test–learn thinking into discussions of societal applications.

### 3.3 May 17 | First Tsinghua University Synthetic Biology Creative Design Competition

On May 17, 2026, we entered the First Tsinghua University Synthetic Biology Creative Design Competition. We proposed **an allosterically regulated conditional RNA-cleavage system for selective intervention against abnormal mRNA in Huntington's disease** and received **third prize**.
