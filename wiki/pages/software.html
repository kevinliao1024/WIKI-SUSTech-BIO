1.1Overview

The whole-body model connects delivery, tissue distribution, cellular expression and RNA editing in a single dynamical model. It asks how losses along the delivery pathway and competing editing reactions shape the final APOE RNA profile.

An editor's activity in a cellular assay does not describe the entire delivery-to-editing process. Editor availability in brain cells depends on tissue distribution and clearance, blood–brain barrier transport, cellular uptake and expression. Each process can alter how much editor reaches its substrate and how long it remains available. Meanwhile, additional editing can convert an intended APOE3-like product into a different RNA state. We therefore needed to relate delivery and exposure to both product composition and unwanted editing, beyond the aggregate editing yield.

We built the whole-body model to examine these connected processes under a shared set of assumptions. It allows us to compare delivery, expression and reaction parameters and identify which processes most strongly affect the outputs. Dose scans ask whether increasing input improves the intended product or mainly increases competing reactions. The accompanying tissue simulation asks whether a higher average editing fraction also provides more even coverage across cells at different distances from a vessel.

Programmable PUF–APOBEC editing provides the biological starting point (Han et al., 2022). The model extends this concept with explicit transport and reaction assumptions.

Our modeling contribution connects systemic delivery, intracellular expression and explicit multisite RNA reactions within one interpretable framework. A complementary spatial model examines tissue coverage. This design distinguishes intended-product retention, unwanted-edit burden and spatial coverage, allowing each to inform a different engineering decision.

In the saved 168-hour dose scan, APOE3-like RNA reached about 46% at normalized doses of 0.7–1.0 before declining at higher doses. In the 72-hour spatial simulations, a tenfold dose increase raised mean editing nearly tenfold but retained an approximately fourfold near-vessel versus far-vessel gap. These model-derived findings distinguish two effects that a single editing-efficiency value would miss. More editing need not preserve more of the intended product, and a higher tissue average need not provide more even coverage.

The reproducible software makes these modeling decisions inspectable and reusable. We share the ODE source, configuration and parameter records, frozen plotting inputs and viewers for saved outputs. These resources let other teams reproduce the supplied ODE workflows and regenerate figures from saved data. Readers can trace figures to their inputs, compare assumptions and inspect the processes behind aggregate results when developing their own models.

1.2Modeling approach

Delivery and expression determine editor availability

The input specifies dose, route and a parameter set. Compartment balances track absorption, exchange between blood and tissues, clearance, barrier transport and intracellular expression. AAV distribution modeling provides a basis for this compartmental representation (Liu et al., 2024).

Each state changes through incoming and outgoing fluxes, production and loss. The coupled ordinary differential equations share a state vector, so upstream delivery changes propagate to editor availability.

dxi/dt = Σ Jin − Σ Jout + Pi − Li

Barrier transport includes uptake, recycling, degradation and release into brain interstitial space. Intracellular uptake and nuclear entry then drive transcription and translation, balanced by RNA and protein degradation.

Competing reactions determine the RNA products

PUF recognition motivates explicit binding, dissociation and catalytic reactions (Wang et al., 2002; Han et al., 2022). The multisite model follows RNA states corresponding to APOE residues 112 and 158.

Editing at 112 produces the APOE3-like state; editing at both positions produces the APOE2-like state. The isoform labels follow the corresponding amino-acid differences (Weisgraber et al., 1981). The 158-only product, bound complexes and RNA turnover remain explicit.

Three branches track unwanted editing: local bystander events, PUF-mismatch editing and deaminase background (Grünewald et al., 2019).

Outputs distinguish product yield from specificity

The reported on-target fraction combines APOE3-like and APOE2-like RNA. The desired APOE3-like fraction is also reported separately. Both use the total free and bound APOE RNA pool as their denominator.

Off-target burden normalizes the three accumulated unwanted-edit outputs by the same RNA pool. Specificity divides the combined edited product by those unwanted-edit outputs, with a small numerical stabilizer. The model tracks the APOE2-like fraction as its LDLR-binding risk proxy.

Dose scans compare these outputs with exposure and existing model criteria. Local sensitivity perturbs parameters, while sampled parameter sets quantify uncertainty. The compartmental ODE chain uses SciPy's LSODA solver (Virtanen et al., 2020).

1.3Results

Across the whole-body, cellular and tissue simulations, four stories turn model outputs into design choices. All dose comparisons use normalized model inputs.

  1. More dose can erase the gain

    The dose response unfolds in three regimes: product accumulation, a plateau, and overediting. At 168 h, APOE3-like RNA reaches about 46% at doses 0.7–1.0, peaking at 0.9, then declines. At dose 10, APOE2-like RNA outnumbers the desired APOE3-like product 3.5 to 1. The second edit consumes molecules that had already reached the intended state (Figures 4 and 6).

    Every scanned dose misses at least one joint criterion. The result makes design improvement the next priority, rather than continued dose escalation.

  2. The dual-site trap

    Targeting both positions, 112 and 158, sounds more complete. At matched dose 2 and 72 h, the dual-site candidate's APOE2-like fraction is about 170 times that of the single-site 10R A3A comparator. Its APOE3-like yield is also lower: 4.44% versus 5.52%. The single-site candidate wins on both measures, placing the dual-site design behind the Pareto front (Figure 8B).

    Preserving the intended product makes single-site selectivity a design priority.

  3. Expression and delivery dominate specificity

    At the local sensitivity screen's reference state, a 20% increase in transcription, translation or brain-entry rates changes specificity roughly 8–9 times more strongly than the same increase in on-target catalysis. These upstream increases reduce specificity, revealing the cost of excess editor exposure (Figure 8A).

    Editor mRNA and protein time courses become the next priority: tune when and how much editor is available before pursuing faster catalysis.

  4. Tenfold more dose, the same fourfold gap

    At 72 h, brain cells in the nearest vessel-distance quartile have about four times the mean editing fraction of cells in the farthest quartile. This gap persists at all three doses: 0.3, 1 and 3. Raising the dose tenfold raises mean editing almost tenfold, yet leaves the spatial imbalance intact (Figures 3 and 8C).

    The tissue story shifts the design goal toward broader coverage: reach the distant cells as well as those beside the vessel.

PhysiCell places individual cells within a three-dimensional tissue volume, and BioFVM resolves extracellular transport (Ghaffarizadeh et al., 2018; 2016).

PUFFER

2.1Overview

PUFFER provides a browser workspace for selecting RNA recognition windows, assembling PUF candidates and exporting records for local analysis. A saved project keeps the target sequence, candidate proteins and measured assay records together.

The website and its companion local application connect two workflows from our Model page: Module 2's assay-informed PUF specificity ranking and Module 4's LAMAR-based RNA-site prioritization. The browser organizes target sequences, PUF candidates and assay records; the local application performs the supported protein-ranking and RNA-context analyses. The outputs prioritize candidate proteins and RNA sites, respectively, and remain separate in the exported results.

PUF repeat recognition provides the basis for assigning RNA-binding motifs (Wang et al., 2002; Zhou et al., 2021). The interface supports complete built-in 8R and 12R scaffolds; other lengths require compatible scaffold input.

2.3Modeling approach

Protein sequence supports candidate ranking

The local application sums mutant-minus-reference masked log-probability differences in the original reference sequence to obtain an ESM-2 score (Lin et al., 2023). Frozen Ridge regressors combine the standardized score with mutation indicators to predict the team's C295, C388 and C871 assay readouts (Hoerl and Kennard, 1970).

The ranking score subtracts the mean predicted C295 and C871 readouts from predicted C388. The fitted coefficients and scoring definition come from the team's assay data.

This model applies to the supplied 492/493-amino-acid scaffold and fixed assays. Incompatible proteins remain unscored; the browser itself exports unscored design candidates.

RNA context supports site prioritization

LAMAR represents RNA sequence context using a pretrained RNA language model (Zhou et al., 2025). The local application uses an adapted model to prioritize cytidines within RNA windows.

The RNA-context score is independent of the selected PUF and is not an editing probability. Protein ranking and RNA-site ranking therefore remain separate outputs.

2.4Workflow

  1. InputDefine the target

    Enter RNA, DNA or a single-record FASTA sequence. Select a cytidine using 1-based coordinates and inspect 8–12-nucleotide recognition windows.

  2. DesignSave the candidates

    Review repeat assignments, build or import candidate proteins, and retain the project as JSON.

  3. AnalysisRun the local models

    Export candidate CSV and supply a species FASTA to the local application. Keep ranking tables, site outputs and the run manifest together.

Additional exports include protein FASTA, matching-site TSV and a local HTML report. The run manifest records input and model hashes, software versions and completion status.
