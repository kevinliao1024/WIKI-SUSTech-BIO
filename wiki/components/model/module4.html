4.1Overview

Programmable RNA editing systems must balance desired target activity with control over unintended transcriptome-wide editing. RNA-seq can identify experimentally supported editing events, but experimentally testing every cytidine in the human transcriptome is impractical.

To address this challenge, we developed a LAMAR-based computational workflow to prioritize potential off-target C-to-U editing sites generated by our PUF-LIKER-APOBEC RNA editing system. The workflow integrates RNA-seq-derived editing evidence, human-transcriptome adaptation of an RNA foundation model and a two-stage prediction strategy.

Our objective is to rank cytidines within human transcript regions for targeted follow-up of potential C-to-U off-target editing. The intended decision is which sites to measure next under a defined editor and experimental condition.

This task presents two central challenges.

First, experimentally observed editing sites are rare relative to the total number of cytidines in the human transcriptome. A direct classifier would therefore encounter severe class imbalance.

Second, a cytidine not detected by RNA-seq cannot automatically be considered truly non-editable. A potential site may not be observed because of limited coverage, low transcript expression, low editing efficiency, sequencing sensitivity or the specific conditions used in the current experiment. Treating every unobserved cytidine as a confirmed negative label would introduce uncertain negatives into the training data.

We therefore designed the system as a prioritization framework. Rather than attempting to make an absolute edited/non-edited decision across the entire transcriptome, the workflow first enriches candidate transcript regions and then ranks cytidines within those regions. Our model provides a ranked candidate list that helps reduce the experimental search space for downstream validation.

Within the project, RNA-seq analysis establishes candidate editing evidence, while PUF design and APOBEC engineering examine changes to the editor. This module addresses the complementary task of choosing RNA sites for further assessment. Its present evidence comes from saved computational partitions; validation of newly prioritized sites remains prospective.

4.2Model and training data

LAMAR foundation model

LAMAR is a Transformer-based RNA foundation model designed to learn regulatory information directly from RNA sequences. It uses a bidirectional Transformer encoder architecture related to ESM-style language models. The model contains 12 Transformer layers, 12 self-attention heads, a hidden dimension of 768 and rotary positional embeddings.

Self-attention lets the representation of a nucleotide depend on other positions in the input, following the Transformer framework (Vaswani et al., 2017). Rotary position embeddings encode positional relationships within that attention calculation (Su et al., 2021). Together, these components let LAMAR represent a cytidine in its surrounding sequence context rather than as an isolated base.

LAMAR uses single-nucleotide tokenization with the vocabulary {A, T, C, G, N} together with special tokens including <cls>, <eos>, <mask> and <pad>. The LAMAR-2k configuration supports contexts up to 2,048 nucleotides, making it suitable for transcript-region prediction.

The original model was pretrained with masked language modeling on approximately 15 million RNA sequences from 225 mammalian species and 1,569 mammal-infecting viral species. The pretraining corpus included mRNAs, non-coding RNAs and annotated genes from RefSeq and RNAcentral. After clustering at 80% sequence identity, the corpus contained approximately 267 billion nucleotide tokens.

The underlying sequence resources serve distinct roles: RefSeq provides annotated reference sequences, while RNAcentral integrates non-coding RNA sequences from specialist databases. These references describe the resources used by the published foundation model; our human-transcript adaptation uses the GENCODE inputs specified below.

LAMAR has previously been fine-tuned for multiple RNA regulatory tasks, including splice-site prediction, translation-efficiency prediction, RNA stability prediction and internal ribosome entry site activity prediction. We selected LAMAR because its pretrained sequence representations could be adapted to the human transcriptome and then applied to APOBEC-associated RNA editing prediction.

The foundation model and its pretraining procedure were developed by Zhou et al. (2025). Our contribution here is its adaptation to human transcripts and the construction and evaluation of an editing-site prioritization workflow.

RNA-seq-derived training labels

We constructed high-confidence positive labels from RNA-seq candidate sites generated for the PUF-LIKER-APOBEC editing system.

The source manuscript reports that a candidate editing site was retained as a positive example when it met both criteria below. The released positive-site table lacks the read-depth columns needed to reconstruct this filtering.

  • Editing-supporting reads: at least 20

  • Total sequencing depth: at least 100

These thresholds were used to reduce uncertainty among positive labels. However, cytidines outside this positive-site set were not interpreted as experimentally proven non-editable sites. They represent unobserved sites under the current experimental conditions.

The distinction between observed positives and unlabeled examples is formalized in positive–unlabeled learning (Elkan and Noto, 2008). We use that distinction to explain the uncertainty in our labels. The present workflow does not implement their probability correction, and its assumption that labeled positives are sampled randomly cannot be presumed for coverage-dependent RNA-seq detection.

All sequences were represented in transcript orientation. Because the editor performs C-to-U editing, cytidines were used as the candidate nucleotides for cytidine-level prediction.

Human transcriptome continued pretraining

Rationale

The original LAMAR model was pretrained using RNA sequences from many mammalian and viral species. Our RNA editing system was evaluated on human transcripts, which may differ in nucleotide composition, transcript architecture and local sequence contexts.

We therefore performed continued unsupervised pretraining on human transcript sequences before downstream editing-site prediction.

Continued pretraining has been studied as a way to adapt a broadly pretrained language model to a particular domain (Gururangan et al., 2020). That study concerned natural language; here it provides a methodological rationale. Whether adaptation improves RNA editing-site prediction must be assessed with the downstream results, separately from masked-nucleotide accuracy.

Transcript annotations came from GENCODE human release 50, and sequence extraction used GffRead (Pertea and Pertea, 2020). The reference version and input filenames below specify the sequence resource used in this workflow.

Mudge et al. (2025) describe the GENCODE annotation framework. The release-specific resource identifies the actual version used here, keeping the annotation reference distinct from the general database publication.

Continued pretraining strategy

Starting from the original LAMAR checkpoint, we continued masked language model training on the human transcriptome. During training, selected nucleotide tokens were masked and the model was required to reconstruct them from their surrounding sequence context.

Masked language modeling learns from surrounding context without requiring task-specific labels, as established for bidirectional language models by Devlin et al. (2019). In this module the tokens are nucleotides and the checkpoint is LAMAR. Learning to reconstruct a nucleotide and learning to rank an editing site remain separate objectives.

For example:


Input:      AUGCUA\[MASK]GGUA

Prediction: AUGCUAACGGUA

This process adapts the pretrained representations toward sequence patterns observed in human transcripts without requiring editing-site labels.

Continued pretraining result

The source manuscript reports the following masked nucleotide prediction accuracies on human transcript sequences.

Saved evaluation logs support the final masked prediction accuracy of 0.575. The original-model baseline of 0.505 remains source-reported because its evaluation record is absent.

Continued pretraining and editing-site training address different objectives for the same RNA input. Masked nucleotide prediction measures reconstruction of sequence context, while supervised training uses RNA-seq-derived editing labels. Downstream evaluation is needed to establish the value of adaptation for candidate prioritization.

This module addresses the gap between many RNA-seq-supported candidates and limited experimental follow-up capacity. Its working assumption is that sequence context contains information useful for ranking sites within the observed dataset. The intended two-stage design separates region assessment from within-region ranking. Its design role is to organize future measurements; the present records support a computational assessment, while prospective benefit and complete workflow performance remain unestablished.

4.3Two-stage prediction

Initial strategy: direct cytidine-level classification

Our first design treated experimentally observed editing sites as positive examples and other transcriptomic cytidines as negative examples.

The intended task was:


Input:  101-nt RNA sequence centered on a cytidine

Output: Edited / not edited

The source manuscript reports poor performance for direct nucleotide-level classification and identifies two likely reasons.

Severe class imbalance

Experimentally observed editing sites were rare compared with all cytidines in the transcriptome. The training data therefore contained far more nominal negative examples than positive examples.

Under these conditions, a model can obtain apparently favorable accuracy by predicting the majority class while failing to identify the rare sites that matter experimentally. Precision-recall metrics are more appropriate than accuracy for this setting, but the underlying imbalance remains difficult.

Precision–recall evaluation makes recovery of positive sites explicit, and its interpretation depends on the proportion of positives in the evaluated data (Saito and Rehmsmeier, 2015). We therefore report region-level and cytidine-level performance separately.

Uncertain negative labels

An unobserved cytidine is not necessarily a non-editable cytidine. Potential editing sites can remain undetected because of insufficient sequencing depth, low transcript abundance, stochastic sampling, low editing frequency or experimental conditions.

Therefore, using all unobserved cytidines as negative examples risks teaching the model to distinguish currently detected sites from currently undetected sites, rather than learning the biological sequence features associated with editability.

A model that perfectly reproduces existing RNA-seq labels would not necessarily discover previously unmeasured off-target sites. This motivated a redesign from transcriptome-wide binary classification to a two-stage candidate-prioritization workflow.

Stage I: 1024-nt candidate-region classifier

The first model identifies transcript regions that are more likely to contain at least one editing site.

The source manuscript describes overlapping 1,024-nt windows with a 128-nt overlap. Released records include shorter regions, and the construction code and transcript identifiers are unavailable.

A window was labeled positive when it contained at least one high-confidence editing site. A window containing no detected editing site was labeled negative.

The source manuscript describes a transcript-level split, but the released records contain only sequences and labels. Transcript-disjoint partitioning cannot therefore be verified.

The versioned evidence audit found one shared full region sequence between training and validation and two between training and test. Candidate sequence contexts also overlap across partitions; the reported evaluation is not sequence-disjoint.

Related examples crossing a split can inflate apparent generalization, a broader reproducibility concern discussed by Kapoor and Narayanan (2023). We interpret the reported scores as development evidence on the supplied partitions, without claiming independence of transcript contexts.

The Stage I task was formulated as:


Input:  1,024-nt transcript sequence window

Output: Probability that the window contains at least one RNA-seq-supported editing site

Stage II: 101-nt cytidine-centered ranking

The second model evaluates individual cytidines within the candidate regions.

The source manuscript describes a cytidine-centered classifier using 101-nt contexts. The retained Stage II code instead assigns a scalar score to the central cytidine and trains it for ranking.


Output: Editing-associated score

In the intended application, Stage II ranks cytidines within regions prioritized by Stage I. The archived ranking evaluation does not apply this Stage I selection step.

The intended hierarchical strategy has two practical advantages:

  • Stage I reduces the transcriptome-wide search space by enriching candidate regions.

  • Stage II focuses scoring on cytidines from enriched regions, where experimental screening is more practical.

The workflow does not eliminate all uncertain negative labels, but it avoids treating transcriptome-wide cytidines as equally relevant during final candidate prioritization.

The two stages divide one large search into two decisions at different resolutions. A positive Stage I label means that a window contains at least one supported site, so the model first evaluates the surrounding region. Stage II recenters the sequence on each candidate cytidine and assigns a separate score to that position. Ranking these scores turns a selected region into an ordered list of sites that can be measured within an available screening budget.

4.4Results

Stage I results: candidate-region enrichment

The best Stage I search result was 0.813, while the saved final validation report records 0.810. Archived test predictions support the reported test value of 0.722. The historical metric named PR-AUC is average precision (AP); the table retains the original reported values.

The test set contains 140 positive windows among 212 windows, a positive fraction of approximately 66.0%. This composition matters when interpreting PR-AUC: the evaluation concerns an already enriched set of transcript windows. The reported score does not establish performance across an unscreened transcriptome, and matched simple baselines remain important for assessing the added value of the model.

Dataset Best PR-AUC
Validation 0.813
Test 0.722

These results describe performance on the supplied region partitions. Sequence overlap and missing transcript identities limit their interpretation as evidence of generalization.

The continued-pretrained and original LAMAR models showed lower training losses than encoder-frozen and randomly initialized conditions. Lower training loss alone does not establish improved performance on unseen transcript contexts.

The saved histories do not establish uniform superiority of continued pretraining. Original LAMAR with a frozen encoder has the highest logged validation AP among these single-run records, as documented in the evidence audit.

Stage II results: cytidine ranking

The source manuscript reports cytidine-level PR-AUC around 0.2. Corresponding prediction and metric files are absent, so this value has not been independently reconstructed.

However, PR-AUC alone does not fully represent the intended use of this module. Our practical goal is to prioritize a short list of cytidines for wet-lab testing after candidate-region enrichment.

The source manuscript reports the ranking summaries below. Retained checkpoint metadata supports validation Hit@10 of 0.8467, but per-candidate scores and complete ranking outputs are absent. The remaining summaries have not been independently reconstructed.

Ranking metrics

For a given candidate region:

  • Hit@K indicates whether at least one true editing site was present among the top K highest-scoring cytidines.

  • Recall@K indicates the fraction of true editing sites recovered among the top K cytidines.

  • MRR is the mean reciprocal rank of the highest-ranked true editing site.

  • Best rank is the rank of the highest-scoring true editing site within a region.

The source-reported test summary places at least one supported site within the top 5 cytidines in 60.7% of evaluated positive regions. The corresponding proportions are 77.9% for the top 10 and 92.1% for the top 20.

The source-reported median best rank was 4. This statistic describes the highest-ranked labelled positive within the evaluated positive regions.

Here, Hit@10 describes whether at least one RNA-seq-supported site occurs within a region's first ten candidates. It is not the fraction of those ten candidates that are edited, nor the fraction of all transcriptome-wide sites recovered. These rankings support a shortlist for evaluation within the reported regions; they do not yet measure the complete Stage I-to-Stage II workflow on new transcripts.

4.5Interpretation and next steps

The two stages address different prediction problems.

Stage I addresses candidate-region enrichment, with archived test predictions supporting AP of 0.722 on the supplied partition. The historical input-context discrepancy and split-overlap limitations remain unresolved.

Stage II ranks individual cytidines within a candidate region. The source-reported classification results do not establish high-confidence binary classification at single-nucleotide resolution.

The source-reported ranking results motivate further assessment of candidate prioritization. A supported site occurred within the top 10 candidates in 77.9% of evaluated test positive regions and within the top 20 in 92.1%.

A ranked shortlist could focus experimental follow-up on fewer cytidines. Its practical benefit remains prospective because the complete two-stage workflow has not been evaluated on newly measured sites.

Engineering implications

This module creates a computational bridge between transcriptome-wide RNA-seq measurements and iterative editor engineering.

Potential uses and extensions include:

  • prioritization of potential off-target cytidines for targeted validation;

  • future comparison of variant-specific off-target candidate profiles, subject to variant-resolved training and evaluation;

  • identification of transcript regions requiring additional experimental screening;

  • expansion of RNA-seq-derived training data through newly validated sites;

  • future design cycles incorporating sequence, transcript abundance and RNA structure features.

The current model should be interpreted as a candidate-ranking system. Experimental validation remains necessary to establish whether high-scoring sites are edited in a specific cell type, editor configuration and treatment condition.

The uses listed above extend beyond what the present evaluation establishes. In particular, the current sequence-only input does not encode which PUF or APOBEC variant is applied. Comparing editor variants would require variant-resolved training evidence and evaluation; identical RNA input alone cannot establish different variant-specific editing risks.

The ranking results make the screening budget an explicit design choice. Hit@K asks whether that budget finds any supported site in a region, whereas Recall@K asks what fraction of supported sites it recovers. The test results show greater recovery when the list expands from ten to twenty cytidines, at the cost of more measurements per region. A next experiment could lock one such budget and compare the model's ranked list with random and simple sequence-based selections. Measuring those candidates under a defined editor condition would test the value of the ranking and supply new evidence for subsequent refinement.

Future work

The next iteration will use experimentally tested candidate sites to improve both the positive and negative training evidence. Priority improvements include:

  • validating high-scoring and low-scoring cytidines from held-out candidate regions;

  • incorporating newly confirmed off-target sites into the training dataset;

  • evaluating editor-specific models for different PUF and APOBEC variants;

  • integrating transcript abundance, local RNA secondary structure and accessibility features;

  • testing performance across independent biological replicates and cell contexts;

  • evaluating calibration and precision at experimentally feasible screening thresholds.

4.6Reproducibility and availability

The versioned Module 4 release provides supplied datasets, historical code, saved Stage I predictions and checkpoint metadata. The upstream LAMAR repository provides the foundation-model source and checkpoints.

The source manuscript specifies GENCODE v50, GRCh38 and positive-label thresholds of at least 20 editing-supporting reads and total depth of at least 100. These source specifications are preserved; the release does not contain all inputs needed to reconstruct their application.

The versioned evidence audit links reported claims to saved records and documents configuration differences, split overlap and missing Stage II outputs. Large weights and reference inputs remain outside this release. Transcript identifiers, the split builder, tokenized cache and pinned training environment are unavailable, so this is an evidence release rather than a complete executable predictor.
